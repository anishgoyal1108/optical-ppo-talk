Hi everyone. This is optical PPO: Pasha’s scheme for training a Kerr-ring reservoir with reinforcement learning, built on work by Pasha, Lida, Shi-Yuan, Mahmoud and Mohammad.

Three control games. CartPole: push the cart left or right so the pole stays up. This is the trained ring playing.

The whole system is one loop. The game hands us its state: for CartPole, four numbers.

Mohammad asked for this as a reservoir-computing problem with the ring as a black box. Six parts: inputs and outputs, the encoding, the reservoir, training the readout, why the reservoir helps, and what’s next.

First, what the game gives us and what we give back: a few numbers in, one of a few moves out.

CartPole’s whole state is four numbers: cart position and speed, pole angle and how fast it turns. Each bar is in units of that number’s typical size.

The other end: the game wants one move back. For CartPole, push left or right. The readout gives one probability per move. The pushes are scripted; the bars end on the ring’s real odds.

Put the ends together. CartPole: four tones in beside a pump, seventeen comb lines read, and one matrix turns them into two odds. Thirty-six numbers: all we train.

Part two, the encoding: how a handful of numbers becomes light. Squash each number, then write it onto its own tone.

Back to the cart-pole’s four numbers. Nothing stops a number from getting big, so before it touches the light it goes through this curve, a hyperbolic tangent.

Now the light. A frequency axis with numbered comb lines. Line zero carries the pump, always on. Lines one to four get one number each.

Why the plus one? Without it the amplitude follows the number, sign and all. Tilt the pole left, then right: the amplitude flips sign, but a detector reads power, so both poses look the same.

Part three, the reservoir. The box stays closed, as Mohammad asked; I open it only far enough to see where products of the inputs come from.

This is all we use about the reservoir. Five lines of light go in: the pump and one tone per number. A comb comes out, and we read seventeen lines.

The comb again, on a log scale. We drive lines zero to four. With the Kerr effect off the ring is a plain filter: light stays where we put it.

The simplest case: the pump and two tones, on lines one and three. Kerr on: one photon steps down from line one to the pump, one steps up from line three, and line four lights up though nothing drives it.

Reading the box: a photodiode behind each line gives its power. One frame of the comb is seventeen numbers, the feature vector phi. Red is above a line’s usual value, blue below.

Part four, the centre: how the one trained piece, the readout, learns. The method is PPO: first what that is, then exactly what it does to the weights.

Reinforcement learning in one picture. The agent sees the state, has a probability for each move, draws one, and gets a point per step the pole stays up. This one knows nothing: down in thirteen steps. Slowed six times.

Here is the whole policy. The box hands us seventeen line powers: call that vector phi, one number under each comb line.

Why PPO. The oldest recipe, the plain policy gradient: record a tape, take one small step towards the moves that paid off, throw the tape away. On our own system, five runs: it gets there, slowly.

How PPO works, in five steps. Step one: play. Sixty-four games side by side, each with its own copy of the ring. This is the real tape of the fourth update.

Step two: score every frame. Take one game off the tape. Replayed here: the pole falls partway along and a new game starts.

Step three, the policy step. Three real frames: the readout’s odds, the move it drew, and the advantage: better than expected, about as expected, worse.

Step four, the leash. Per frame, one number: the new probability of its move over the old one. For a good frame the plain rule says the bigger the better, without limit.

Step five. The same tape is used twenty times; each pass moves W a little. The ring is not asked again: the passes are arithmetic on stored numbers.

Now the part that matters most: what exactly happens to W. Here is W on this tape, two rows of seventeen, and the tape itself. Take one frame off it.

The whole training run, the real one. W starts as all zeros, so the agent pushes at random and the pole drops. Slowed three times.

One last thing, and it is the piece that is special to optics. Forwards, the state goes through the ring once and the spectrum is written to the tape.

Part five: why bother with the ring at all. The readout is only a linear map, so what it can do depends on what the comb hands it.

Start with XOR: two inputs, red where they agree, blue where they differ. A linear readout draws one straight line, and at least one point is always on the wrong side.

Monday’s question, in the pendulum’s state space: angle across, speed up. A readout linear in the inputs gives one torque on each side of its border. Scramble its weights and the border only tilts and shifts.

The game that needs it: the trained ring, starting from hanging. At the bottom the torque points along the motion, so each swing gains energy. On the right, the same motion in the plane of angle and speed.

What each readout learned. The linear one’s torque map: red is counter-clockwise, blue clockwise. Its pendulum sits in the red and circles near the bottom.

Keep that red bar and ask where the ring makes it. Four-wave mixing takes a photon each from lines one and three and puts them on zero and four: cosine theta times theta dot. Two photons from line two can land there too.

Pendulum: linear readout left, ring right, same start, same random draws. At update zero both push at random. Every replay is real, at double speed.

The lander, and Shi-Yuan’s question: does it need a nonlinearity at all? At update zero both crash. At update 25 the ring lands slowly and the linear one touches down but keeps firing.

CartPole, the honest counterexample. At update zero both fall in a quarter of a second. A fallen pole is only twelve degrees over: that is where the game stops.

Last part: what comes next. A direct test of the box’s nonlinearity, where this scheme fits and where it doesn’t, and how to train more than the readout.

First, a direct test of the nonlinearity: Mohammad’s XOR. Two tones, each switched between a low and a high amplitude: four inputs through the same box, four spectra.

Where does this fit? Every input gets its own tone. CartPole: four tones beside the pump, seventeen lines read, ten pair products.

Two more directions. Today only the readout learns, in red. The critic and the softmax are still electronics: both are candidates to move into the optics.

Preliminary, also from October. With more inputs the pair products get harder to read out. Consecutive tones collide: lines one plus four and two plus three feed the same new line. Placing the tones so that no two pairs collide helps until detector noise wins, and it costs more lines. Drafted with AI help, not re-derived yet.

Preliminary, from my ring-requirements study. How many lines does the readout need? For Pendulum, five to nine lines picked by a lasso do as well as all seventeen. LunarLander lands from about thirteen. A few seeds only, and not re-derived yet.

Preliminary. What if the phases of the input tones drift? The original ring, black, tolerates a tenth of a radian and fails when the tones are unlocked. With collision-free tones and training without a phase lock, red, it does not care. Three seeds, not re-derived yet.

Preliminary. Turn the pump down and see when the policy breaks. With one percent relative detector noise it barely matters. With shot and receiver noise it depends on how long the detector integrates: a microsecond holds much further down than a third of a nanosecond. Turning this into watts assumes a one square micron mode area and an estimated coupling g zero, so I do not quote watts. Not re-derived yet.

Preliminary, from my optical-critic study. Can the critic read the comb as well? A critic on the actor’s lines clearly beats having no critic, but the MLP critic still wins. I make no claim from this yet.

Preliminary. The same question with Lida’s lattice as the critic’s reservoir. Lower is better here. On Pendulum it beats a plain linear critic, but most of that comes from remembering the previous step, not from the Kerr effect. On LunarLander nothing separates.
